one_liner: The BabyLM Challenge asks whether a language model can be pretrained well from

claims:
- id: the-budget-is-text-not-compute
  text: The BabyLM Challenge constrains only the quantity of training text -- about 10M words
    in Strict-small and about 100M words in Strict -- and places no limit at all on parameter
    count, compute, hyperparameters or number of epochs.
  scope: This is the 2023 (first) edition's rule set. The constraint is a budget on the data
    a model sees, not on the resources used to train it, so a submission may spend arbitrarily
    many passes and arbitrarily large models on the fixed corpus.
  evidence: Sections 3, 6 and the FAQ (limits on hyperparameters, limits on epochs)
- id: why-under-100m-words
  text: 'The under-100M-word ceiling comes from a developmental estimate, not from a compute
    budget: children are exposed to 2M-7M words per year, so taking age 12 as a cutoff puts
    a child''s cumulative input at 24M-84M words.'
  scope: An order-of-magnitude argument for where to put the ceiling, citing Gilkerson et
    al. (2017) for the per-year rate. The 100M figure is the round number above that range,
    not a measured quantity, and 10M is a subsample rather than a second developmental milestone.
  evidence: Section 4, first bullet
- id: the-corpus-is-mostly-transcribed-speech
  text: The released corpus is deliberately weighted toward transcribed speech rather than
    written text, because most of the language a child hears is spoken -- the largest single
    component is OpenSubtitles at 31% of words.
  scope: '''Mostly transcribed speech'' is about the proportion of speech-like registers across
    ten sources, not about child-directed speech specifically: CHILDES, the corpus of actual
    child-directed speech, is 5%. Subtitles and dialogue transcripts are speech in register
    but were not addressed to a child.'
  evidence: Table 1; Section 4, second bullet
- id: what-is-in-the-fixed-corpus
  text: 'The fixed corpus totals 9.96M words (Strict-small) and 98.04M words (Strict) drawn
    from ten sources: OpenSubtitles 31%, Simple Wikipedia 15%, QED educational subtitles 11%,
    Project Gutenberg 10%, Wikipedia 10%, the BNC dialogue portion 8%, the Children''s Book
    Test 6%, CHILDES 5%, the Children''s Stories Text Corpus 3% and Switchboard 1%.'
  scope: Word counts are of each corpus's training split as included, and the proportions
    are of the mixture rather than of each source's full size. Strict-small is an approximately
    10% uniform subsample of Strict, so the two tracks share a composition and differ only
    in scale.
  evidence: Table 1; Section 3
- id: three-tracks-and-what-each-relaxes
  text: 'The challenge has three tracks: Strict and Strict-small require training exclusively
    on the provided corpus at ~100M and ~10M words, while Loose keeps the 100M-word text ceiling
    but allows different text, unlimited non-linguistic data, and unlimited additional text
    if that text was generated by a model trained under the restrictions.'
  scope: 'Loose relaxes which data, not how much text: the 100M-word ceiling still applies
    to human-authored text. The 2023 edition has no multimodal track of its own -- non-text
    modalities enter through Loose.'
  evidence: Section 3
- id: the-two-strict-tracks-are-ranked-and-loose-is-judged
  text: Winners of Strict and Strict-small are determined by performance on the shared evaluation
    set alone, whereas Loose winners are selected holistically on evaluation performance together
    with relevance to the task's goals, potential impact and novelty.
  scope: Two different selection procedures, which is what makes a Loose result not comparable
    to a Strict ranking. Computational efficiency may also enter the Loose judgement, and
    the organisers reserve a special award for a submission strong in either the engineering
    or the cognitive-science direction but not both.
  evidence: Sections 3, 5 and 6
- id: pretrained-models-are-banned-outright-in-the-strict-tracks
  text: Strict and Strict-small submissions may not use a pretrained model for any purpose
    whatsoever, including reranking and data augmentation, rather than merely being barred
    from initialising from one.
  scope: 'Stated in the FAQ on permitted training regimes, and the broad reading is the point:
    any training objective or regime is allowed as long as no external pretrained model touches
    the pipeline. Loose lifts this for text generated by a compliant model.'
  evidence: FAQ, 'What training regimes are permitted?'; Section 3
- id: multiple-epochs-are-explicitly-allowed
  text: 'The challenge places no limit on the number of epochs, for three stated reasons:
    at this scale SGD needs multiple passes to reach peak performance, humans likewise retain
    and re-learn from a memory of linguistic experience, and the organisers wanted to avoid
    legislating implementations.'
  scope: A deliberate design decision rather than an oversight, and the one most often assumed
    away -- sample-efficient here means efficient in unique words seen, not in tokens processed.
  evidence: FAQ, 'Are there any limits on the number of epochs?'
- id: what-a-submitted-model-must-be-able-to-do
  text: A submission must be loadable and queryable in HuggingFace transformers, must be able
    to score a sequence with a log-likelihood or pseudo-log-likelihood, and must be fine-tunable
    for classification -- but is not required to be able to generate text.
  scope: These are interface requirements imposed by the shared evaluation pipeline, which
    is why encoder-only models are first-class entrants. Participants whose models are not
    transformers-compatible may run their own pipeline, though the organisers discourage it.
  evidence: Section 5
- id: the-evaluation-pipeline-is-built-to-run-on-a-colab-gpu
  text: The shared evaluation pipeline is distributed as Google Colab notebooks and public
    code so that submissions can be scored on a small GPU, which is part of the challenge's
    stated aim of making pretraining research practical outside large industry groups.
  scope: About the cost of evaluating a submission, not of training one; the organisers separately
    note that pretraining at even these scales remains expensive and promise a resource estimate
    for 10M and 100M words.
  evidence: Sections 5 and 6; Section 1 (democratising pretraining research)
- id: the-baselines-are-naive-by-design
  text: The released baselines are OPT, RoBERTa and T5 hyperparameters taken unchanged from
    the large-scale originals and retrained from scratch on the fixed corpus, and the organisers
    state explicitly that these are not meant to be strong baselines but a naive starting
    point.
  scope: One baseline per architecture family -- decoder-only, encoder-only, encoder-decoder
    -- with no tuning for the new data scale. Beating them is the floor of the task, not a
    result.
  evidence: Section 5.1
- id: this-is-the-call-not-the-findings
  text: 'This paper is the call for papers: it defines the corpus, tracks, rules and evaluation
    interface of the first BabyLM Challenge and reports no results, rankings or submissions.'
  scope: The first edition ran with training data released January 2023, the evaluation pipeline
    in March, results due 15 July and papers due 1 August 2023, presented at CoNLL in partnership
    with CoNLL and CMCL. What participants found is in the separate findings paper for that
    edition, and the rules changed in later editions.
  evidence: Abstract; Section 2 (key dates)

misreadings:
- This paper reports no results. It is the call for papers, so citing it for what the challenge
  found -- which approaches helped, what the winning systems did, how the submissions compared
  to the baselines -- attributes the findings paper's content to the wrong paper.
- The 10M/100M-word limit is a limit on training text, not on model size, compute or training
  steps. A BabyLM-compliant model may be arbitrarily large and trained for arbitrarily long;
  only the corpus it reads is fixed.
- '''Sample-efficient'' does not mean one pass over the data. Epochs are explicitly unlimited,
  and the call gives its reasons -- multiple passes are needed at this scale, and human learners
  also revisit remembered linguistic experience.'
- '''Developmentally plausible'' describes the quantity and register mix, not provenance.
  Wikipedia, Simple Wikipedia and movie subtitles are the largest parts of the corpus; actual
  child-directed speech (CHILDES) is 5% of it.'
- Strict-small is not a curated or higher-quality subset. It is an approximately 10% uniform
  subsample of the Strict corpus, so a Strict-small result is about scale rather than about
  a different kind of data.
- The Loose track is not an unlimited-data track. Its text ceiling is still 100M words; what
  it lifts is the requirement that the text be the provided corpus, plus any limit on non-linguistic
  data and on text generated by a compliant model.
- Beating the released baselines is not itself a finding. They are large-model hyperparameters
  transplanted unchanged to a 10M- or 100M-word corpus, and the organisers say plainly that
  they are a naive starting point.
- Being a shared-task call does not make this a description of one fixed benchmark. The rules,
  corpus and evaluation described here are the 2023 edition's; later BabyLM editions changed
  the tracks and the data, so results across years are not automatically comparable.
terminology:
  BabyLM Challenge: A shared task on pretraining a language model from roughly the quantity
    of language a child hears, run in partnership with CoNLL and CMCL.
  developmentally plausible corpus: A mixed-domain corpus whose size and register mix are
    modelled on a child's input -- under 100M words and weighted toward transcribed speech
    -- not a transcript of any child's actual input.
  sample-efficient pretraining: Getting more out of a fixed quantity of training text, with
    no restriction on compute, parameters or epochs spent on it.
  Strict: The track that fixes training data to the provided ~100M-word corpus, ranked on
    the shared evaluation set alone.
  Strict-small: The same as Strict, on an approximately 10% uniform subsample of the corpus
    (~10M words).
  Loose: The track that keeps the 100M-word text ceiling but allows other text, unlimited
    non-linguistic data, and text generated by a compliant model; judged holistically rather
    than ranked.
  pseudo-log-likelihood: The sequence score a masked language model can provide in place of
    a true log-likelihood, which is what lets encoder-only models be evaluated alongside autoregressive
    ones.
